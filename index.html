Acute Stroke Guide

A bedside reference for acute ischaemic stroke and intracerebral haemorrhage, written directly from the 2026 AHA/ASA guideline and the trials behind it. Works offline. Free and open source.

Read this first

This guide reproduces published recommendations and trial results. It does not know your patient. Every recommendation here is a starting point for a decision made by the treating clinician, in the context of that patient's goals, comorbidity and your institution's protocol. Reperfusion decisions in complex cases belong with stroke neurology.

Nothing here is site-specific: there are no local activation criteria, no local screening tools and no institutional workflows. If your hospital's protocol differs, your hospital's protocol governs.

Where to take the patient

The 2026 change

Earlier guidance was "nearest thrombolytic-capable facility". The 2026 guideline now endorses considering the local system's characteristics and, in the absence of well-functioning systems with rapid interhospital transfer, direct transport to the nearest EVT-capable hospital. Where rapid transfer processes do work well, the drip-and-ship model remains appropriate. RACECAT, the cluster-randomised trial in non-urban Catalonia, did not settle this question.

Mobile stroke units

MSUs are now recommended where available: multiple randomised studies, including B_PROUD and BEST-MSU, show faster thrombolysis and better functional outcomes than conventional EMS for eligible patients. This is a new recommendation in the 2026 guideline.

Section 2 (Stroke Systems of Care and Prehospital Management), 2026 AHA/ASA AIS guideline.

The one test that must precede thrombolysis

Blood glucose COR 1 B-NR

Determine glucose before initiating IVT: severe hypoglycaemia (typically <50 mg/dL) and severe hyperglycaemia (>400 mg/dL) both mimic stroke. Correct it, then reassess.

If disabling deficits persist after correction to normoglycaemia, give thrombolysis COR 1 — abnormal glucose is not a reason to withhold treatment once corrected.

Severity measurement

A stroke severity scale — preferably the NIHSS — is recommended at baseline and after reperfusion therapy COR 1 B-NR. Baseline NIHSS is among the strongest predictors of 30- and 90-day mortality and independence; the 24-hour NIHSS, adjusted for baseline, is the best single predictor of 90-day function. Open the NIHSS calculator →

Known limitations the guideline itself flags: the NIHSS undersamples posterior circulation deficits, carries a left-hemisphere bias, and has only modest interrater reliability. A low NIHSS does not mean a non-disabling stroke.

Consent and shared decision-making

Discuss risks and benefits with the patient or their representative when feasible COR 1 C-EO. When a patient cannot consent (aphasia, confusion) and no representative is immediately available, the guideline states plainly that it is justified to proceed with thrombolysis in an otherwise eligible adult with disabling deficits.

"Core" is a prediction, not a fact

The guideline is explicit about this. Reduced NCCT density, DWI abnormality and low CBF estimates are predictors of infarction, not synonyms for irreversible tissue loss. The large-core thrombectomy trials exist precisely because the old equation was wrong. Treat the number as an estimate with an error bar.

Severe hypodensity threshold — SELECT2 exploratory analysis

A volume ≥26 mL of severe CT hypodensity (defined as ≤26 Hounsfield units, the lower 99% CI of contralateral thalamic grey matter) identified patients who derived no functional benefit from thrombectomy and had increased cerebral oedema and hemicraniectomy rates. Exploratory, not a recommendation — but worth measuring before committing a very large core to the angio suite.

IV thrombolysis: making the decision

Within 4.5 hours the question is not "how high is the NIHSS" but "is this deficit disabling for this person".

The headline COR 1

In adults with disabling deficits regardless of NIHSS score who are eligible within 4.5 hours, treat as fast as possible with tenecteplase 0.25 mg/kg or alteplase 0.9 mg/kg. NCCT alone is sufficient. Do not delay for CTA, CTP or MRI.

Is the deficit clearly disabling?

The operating question, from Table 4 of the guideline: among patients with NIHSS 0–5, if the observed deficits persist, would they still be able to do basic activities of daily living and/or return to work? Basic ADLs are Bathing/dressing, Ambulating, Toileting, Hygiene and Eating — the BATHE mnemonic. Assess the ability to ambulate and to swallow independently. Make the determination with the patient and family.

Typically clearly disabling

  • Complete hemianopsia (NIHSS vision ≥2)
  • Severe aphasia (NIHSS best language ≥2)
  • Severe hemi-attention or extinction to more than one modality (NIHSS extinction ≥2)
  • Any weakness limiting sustained effort against gravity (NIHSS motor ≥2)

May not be clearly disabling

  • Isolated mild aphasia, still communicating meaningfully
  • Isolated facial droop
  • Mild cortical hand weakness, especially nondominant (NIHSS motor 0)
  • Hemisensory loss alone
  • Mild hemimotor or hemisensorimotor loss
  • Mild hemiataxia, still ambulatory

Table 4, adapted from the PRISMS trial operational definition. The guideline stresses that a deficit disabling for one person may not be for another — lower-limb weakness preventing walking scores only 2 on the NIHSS and is unambiguously disabling.

Non-disabling deficits COR 3: No Benefit B-R

In eligible adults presenting within 4.5 h with mild non-disabling deficits (an isolated sensory syndrome, in many cases), IVT is not recommended: it has not shown superiority over dual antiplatelet therapy. A 2023 randomised trial found DAPT noninferior to alteplase in minor non-disabling stroke, with more early neurological deterioration and bleeding in the alteplase arm. Two 2024 meta-analyses agreed, with lower sICH under DAPT. DAPT regimen →

Choice of agent

Either alteplase or tenecteplase is endorsed within the 4.5-hour window. Tenecteplase's single 5-second bolus, versus a 1-hour infusion, simplifies administration, reduces dosing errors and shortens door-to-needle and door-in-door-out times.

Dose matters

Tenecteplase 0.4 mg/kg is the wrong dose in stroke. NOR-TEST 2 was terminated early for worse safety and functional outcomes at 0.4 mg/kg, and an earlier dose-escalation study showed increased sICH. Use 0.25 mg/kg.

Reading OPTION honestly

OPTION was published in February 2026, after the guideline's literature cut-off, and therefore carries no class of recommendation. It is a single open-label trial conducted entirely in China, with blinded endpoint assessment. It is the first randomised evidence for late thrombolysis in non-LVO stroke, and the effect size is modest: NNT ≈ 11 for an excellent outcome, against an absolute sICH increase of 2.8% from a baseline of zero.

Whether to act on it before it appears in a guideline is a local governance decision, not a lookup.

A safety signal worth knowing

Where NCCT hypodensity is larger than the perfusion-defined core, the mismatch is internally inconsistent and sICH risk is higher. Read the plain CT alongside the perfusion maps rather than trusting the automated output alone. All standard contraindications to thrombolysis apply unchanged in the extended window.

If perfusion imaging is unavailable

MRI with DWI–FLAIR mismatch is the guideline-supported alternative for unknown-onset stroke — a DWI lesion with no corresponding FLAIR change indicates a biological onset likely within 4.5 hours. For CT-only selection of wake-up stroke, TWIST showed a favourable direction of effect but was underpowered; the guideline lists this as an open question, especially important for low-resource settings.

Thrombolysis before thrombectomy

Bridging IVT remains standard for IVT-eligible patients. Two Chinese RCTs found EVT alone noninferior; four other trials failed to confirm superiority or noninferiority of EVT alone, favouring combined therapy for reperfusion efficacy and 90-day outcomes. No trial showed increased sICH from adding IVT.

Do not delay EVT after thrombolysis to assess for clinical improvement.

Large-core thrombectomy: what the trials actually showed

Six randomised trials, 1887 patients, pooled in Liu et al (Neurology 2025): EVT improved the ordinal 90-day mRS (generalised OR 1.6, 95% CI 1.4–1.8) and independent ambulation (mRS 0–3, RR 1.9, 95% CI 1.5–2.5), and reduced mortality (RR 0.9, 95% CI 0.8–1.0), despite higher sICH (RR 1.7, 95% CI 1.1–2.7). Benefit fell as onset-to-randomisation time lengthened. For ASPECTS 0–2, EVT increased mRS 0–3 (RR 2.1) and mRS 0–2 (RR 2.8).

Set expectations before you consent the family

Across the large-core trials, 19.5% of EVT patients achieved functional independence and 36.5% independent ambulation — against 7.5% functional independence with medical therapy alone. That is a genuine doubling, and it is a long way from the ~50% independence seen in the small-core era. NNT to prevent one additional instance of functional dependence is 8. The guideline states that a discussion with the patient and family, where feasible, is essential in this population.

Thrombolysis complications

Two protocols worth knowing before you need them, both reproduced from the guideline.

Symptomatic intracranial bleeding within 24 h of IVT

  1. Stop the alteplase infusion, or the tenecteplase push if still in progress.
  2. Emergent labs: CBC, PT (INR), aPTT, fibrinogen, type and cross-match.
  3. Emergent non-enhanced head CT if there is clinical concern.
  4. Cryoprecipitate (includes factor VIII): 10 units infused over 10–30 min, to maintain fibrinogen ≥150 mg/dL. As a rule of thumb, 10 units raises fibrinogen by roughly 50 mg/dL.
  5. Antifibrinolytic: tranexamic acid 1000 mg IV over 10 min, or ε-aminocaproic acid 4–5 g over 1 h followed by 1 g IV until bleeding is controlled (peak onset at 3 h). Potentially useful in all patients, and particularly where blood products are declined or cryoprecipitate is not rapidly available.
  6. Consult haematology and neurosurgery as necessary.
  7. Supportive therapy: blood pressure, ICP, CPP, MAP, temperature and glucose control.

Orolingual angioedema

Airway first

Intubation may not be necessary if oedema is limited to the anterior tongue and lips. Oedema involving the larynx, palate, floor of mouth or oropharynx, or rapid progression within 30 minutes, carries a much higher risk of needing an airway.

Awake fibreoptic intubation is optimal. Nasotracheal intubation risks epistaxis after a thrombolytic; cricothyroidotomy is rarely needed and equally problematic after thrombolysis.

  1. Discontinue the thrombolytic infusion (if alteplase) and hold ACE inhibitors.
  2. Methylprednisolone 125 mg IV.
  3. Diphenhydramine 50 mg IV.
  4. Ranitidine 50 mg IV or famotidine 20 mg IV.
  5. If angioedema continues to increase: epinephrine 0.1% (1 mg/mL) 0.3 mL subcutaneously, or nebulised 0.5 mg.
  6. Icatibant (selective bradykinin B2 receptor antagonist) 30 mg (3 mL) subcutaneously into the abdomen; a further 30 mg may be given at 6-hourly intervals, to a maximum of 3 injections in 24 h.

Spontaneous intracerebral haemorrhage

Different source document

This section is not from the 2026 AIS guideline, which covers ischaemic stroke only. It draws on the 2022 AHA/ASA Guideline for the Management of Patients With Spontaneous Intracerebral Hemorrhage (doi:10.1161/STR.0000000000000407) plus named trials published since. Classes shown are from that 2022 document.

Haematoma expansion happens in the first hours and predicts death. Everything in the acute phase is aimed at that window: pressure, reversal, and a surgical decision.

The first hour

  1. Airway, GCS, NIHSS. Note the exact time of onset and the last dose of any anticoagulant.
  2. Non-contrast CT — volume by ABC/2, intraventricular extension, location (lobar vs deep), midline shift.
  3. Blood pressure — start lowering within 2 hours, reach target within 1 hour.
  4. Reverse the anticoagulant — the single most time-critical drug decision. Reversal table →
  5. Neurosurgical consultation for cerebellar haemorrhage, hydrocephalus, intraventricular extension, or a lobar haematoma of 30–80 mL.
  6. Avoid early prognostication. Do not withdraw care in the first 24 hours; early do-not-resuscitate orders are an independent predictor of death.

Andexanet alfa: what ANNEXA-I showed

530 patients with intracerebral haemorrhage who had taken a factor Xa inhibitor within 15 hours, randomised to andexanet or usual care (85.5% of the usual-care arm received PCC).

  • Haemostatic efficacy 67.0% vs 53.1% (adjusted difference 13.4 points, 95% CI 4.6–22.2, P=0.003)
  • Median reduction in anti-Xa activity 94.5% vs 26.9% (P<0.001)
  • Thrombotic events 10.3% vs 5.6% (difference 4.6 points, 95% CI 0.1–9.2, P=0.048) — ischaemic stroke 6.5% vs 1.5%
  • No appreciable difference in modified Rankin score or 30-day death

Andexanet controls the haematoma better than usual care, at the cost of a real thrombotic signal, and without a demonstrated functional benefit. That trade-off belongs in the decision, not underneath it. NEJM 2024;390:1745–55. doi:10.1056/NEJMoa2313040

Restarting anticoagulation afterwards

A separate decision, made days later, weighing the indication (mechanical valve > AF), the ICH location (lobar and probable amyloid angiopathy carry higher rebleeding risk than deep hypertensive ICH), and the patient's overall trajectory. Involve stroke neurology and cardiology.

How to read the classifications

Every recommendation in this guide carries the class and level of evidence assigned by the guideline writing committee. They mean specific things.

Class of recommendation — how strongly, and in which direction

ClassMeaningTypical phrasing
1 StrongBenefit greatly outweighs risk"is recommended", "should be performed", "is indicated"
2a ModerateBenefit outweighs risk"is reasonable", "can be useful", "can be beneficial"
2b WeakBenefit is greater than or equal to risk"may be reasonable", "might be considered", "usefulness is not well established"
3: No BenefitBenefit equals risk — moderate or strong evidence of no effect"is not recommended", "is not indicated", "is not useful"
3: HarmRisk exceeds benefit"is potentially harmful", "should not be performed"

Level of evidence — how good the underlying data are

LevelBasis
AHigh-quality evidence from more than one randomised trial, or meta-analyses of high-quality RCTs
B-RModerate-quality evidence from one or more randomised trials, or meta-analyses of such
B-NRModerate-quality evidence from one or more well-designed nonrandomised or observational studies
C-LDLimited data — randomised or nonrandomised studies with design or execution limitations, or physiological studies in humans
C-EOExpert opinion, based on clinical experience
Two habits worth keeping

A Class 3 is a real recommendation. "3: No Benefit" and "3: Harm" carry the same strength as a Class 1 — they are strong recommendations to not do something. Intensive BP lowering after successful thrombectomy is Class 3: Harm, Level A: as well evidenced as anything in the document.

Read the whole row, not the chip. A COR 1 recommendation qualified by "age <80, prestroke mRS 0–1, ASPECTS 3–5, no significant mass effect" is Class 1 for that population, and silent about anyone else.

What the guideline does not claim

The AHA/ASA state that guidelines define practices meeting the needs of patients in most, but not all, circumstances, and should not replace clinical judgement — recommendations must be considered in the context of individual patient values, preferences and associated conditions. That framing applies with even more force to a summary such as this one.
